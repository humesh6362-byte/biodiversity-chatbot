# Biodiversity Indicators Knowledge Base

Species richness (the count of distinct species observed in an area) and
species evenness (how evenly individuals are distributed across those
species) together give a more complete biodiversity picture than richness
alone. A site with 20 species dominated 90% by one species is far less
resilient than a site with 15 species evenly distributed, since resilience
depends on functional redundancy across the community.
Source: Convention on Biological Diversity, biodiversity indicator
frameworks.

Pollinator abundance and diversity (bees, butterflies, hoverflies) is widely
used as a fast-responding indicator of habitat quality, because pollinators
react to floral resource availability within a single season, unlike slower
indicators like tree cover. Introducing flowering cover crops or hedgerows
can raise pollinator visitation rates measurably within one growing season,
well before soil-based indicators show change.
Source: FAO Pollination Services and Food Security report.

Soil biodiversity (earthworms, arthropods, fungal and bacterial diversity) is
harder to observe directly but correlates strongly with soil organic carbon
and reduced tillage. Earthworm counts are a commonly used low-cost field
proxy: healthy agricultural soils typically support 8-16 earthworms per
square meter, while heavily tilled, low-SOC soils often support fewer than 2.
Source: FAO Global Soil Biodiversity Atlas.

Habitat diversity (the number of distinct vegetation structures or land
cover types within a landscape, e.g. hedgerow + wetland + woodland edge +
grassland) is a strong predictor of overall species richness because
different species require different structural niches for nesting,
foraging, and shelter across their life cycle. Landscapes with 4 or more
distinct habitat types within a few hundred meters typically support
significantly higher bird and mammal diversity than single-habitat
landscapes of the same total area.
Source: IPBES Global Assessment, landscape-level biodiversity indicators.

Indicator species (species whose presence or absence signals broader
ecosystem condition, e.g. certain amphibians for wetland health, or lichen
diversity for air quality) allow rapid, low-cost biodiversity assessment
without exhaustive species surveys. Selecting 2-3 locally relevant indicator
species is recommended as a practical monitoring strategy for
resource-constrained restoration projects.
Source: CBD monitoring and indicator guidance.

Confidence in biodiversity indicator improvements should generally be rated
higher for near-term, fast-responding indicators (pollinator visitation,
weed/floral diversity — visible within 1 growing season) and lower for
slow-responding indicators (soil fungal diversity, large-mammal population
recovery — often requiring 3-10+ years), since intervention effects compound
more unpredictably over longer timeframes.
Source: FAO and CBD joint guidance on ecological monitoring timeframes.
