# Climate Factors Knowledge Base

Rainfall variability, more than total annual rainfall, is often the primary
constraint on biodiversity and crop productivity in semi-arid regions.
Regions with the same average annual rainfall but higher inter-annual
variability generally show lower species richness, because fewer species
can tolerate both drought and flood extremes within their life cycle.
Source: IPCC AR6 Working Group II, regional climate impact assessments.

In low-rainfall, semi-arid regions, practices that improve soil water
retention (cover cropping, mulching, agroforestry with drought-tolerant
species) have a compounding benefit: they simultaneously improve crop
resilience and expand the window during which soil moisture supports
below-ground biodiversity. Agroforestry with nitrogen-fixing, drought
tolerant trees (e.g. Faidherbia albida in African drylands) has been shown
to increase understory crop yield by 30-50% partly through improved soil
moisture and microclimate buffering.
Source: FAO drylands agroforestry programme; World Agroforestry Centre
Faidherbia albida studies.

Rising temperatures shift species ranges poleward or to higher elevations at
an estimated median rate of several kilometers per decade for many taxa,
but habitat fragmentation prevents many species from actually tracking these
shifts, since they cannot cross agricultural or urban land to reach newly
suitable habitat. This makes landscape connectivity (corridors, buffer
strips, agroforestry belts) an increasingly important climate adaptation
tool, not just a biodiversity one.
Source: IPCC AR6 Working Group II, climate-driven species range shifts.

Climate-smart agriculture practices (intercropping, agroforestry, cover
cropping, reduced tillage) tend to deliver three simultaneous benefits:
increased carbon sequestration, improved resilience to rainfall variability,
and improved biodiversity indicators — making them efficient interventions
where multiple environmental variables are degraded at once, rather than
requiring separate interventions per variable.
Source: FAO Climate-Smart Agriculture Sourcebook.

Short-term climate interventions (e.g. mulching for moisture retention) show
benefits within a single growing season, medium-term interventions (cover
cropping, intercropping) typically show measurable soil and biodiversity
benefits within 2-3 years, and long-term interventions (agroforestry
establishment, large-scale reforestation) generally require 5-10+ years to
reach their full carbon sequestration and habitat potential.
Source: FAO and IPCC joint guidance on intervention timeframes for
climate-agriculture-biodiversity co-benefits.
