# Human Impact Knowledge Base

Agricultural runoff (excess nitrogen and phosphorus from over-fertilization)
is a leading cause of freshwater biodiversity decline, causing algal blooms
that deplete dissolved oxygen and create aquatic dead zones. Reducing
synthetic fertilizer application by 20-30% while substituting legume cover
crops or compost for a portion of nitrogen needs can maintain yield while
substantially reducing runoff-driven biodiversity loss downstream.
Source: FAO nutrient management guidance; EPA nutrient pollution reports.

Deforestation for agricultural expansion remains the dominant land-use
driver of tropical biodiversity loss. Selective, buffer-zone-based
approaches (retaining forest strips around cleared plots, or converting to
agroforestry rather than clear-cutting) can retain a meaningfully higher
share of original species richness — commonly cited estimates suggest
buffer-zone approaches retain 40-60% more forest-dependent species than
complete clearance of the same area.
Source: FAO Forestry Programme; CBD Global Biodiversity Outlook.

Pesticide use, particularly broad-spectrum insecticides, has a documented
disproportionate impact on pollinator populations relative to target pest
species, since pollinators are exposed both directly and through
contaminated pollen and nectar. Integrated Pest Management (IPM) approaches
that combine targeted, lower-toxicity treatments with biological controls
(beneficial insects, crop rotation) can reduce pesticide-driven pollinator
decline while maintaining comparable pest control efficacy.
Source: FAO Integrated Pest Management guidance; Intergovernmental
Science-Policy Platform on Pollinators (IPBES pollination assessment).

Soil and water pollution from industrial or mining runoff tends to have
longer recovery timelines than agricultural nutrient pollution — heavy metal
contamination in particular can persist in soils for decades, meaning
remediation (phytoremediation with hyperaccumulator plants, or soil
replacement) should be prioritized over passive recovery in heavily polluted
sites.
Source: FAO and UNEP joint land degradation and pollution assessments.

Human-impact interventions generally combine well with soil- and
land-use-based interventions: for example, reducing fertilizer input while
adding legume cover crops addresses both a human-impact variable (nutrient
pollution) and a soil-health variable (organic nitrogen, SOC) with a single
practice change, which is often more cost-effective for smallholders than
addressing each variable in isolation.
Source: FAO integrated landscape management guidance.
