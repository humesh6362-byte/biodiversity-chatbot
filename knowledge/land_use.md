# Land Use and Land Cover Knowledge Base

Monoculture cropping systems, especially continuous cereal monocultures like
wheat or maize grown on the same land year after year, are strongly
associated with declining habitat diversity. Monocultures typically support
60-80% fewer arthropod and bird species than adjacent polyculture or
agroforestry plots of comparable size, because they remove the structural
and seasonal diversity that many species rely on for food and shelter.
Source: Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), agricultural biodiversity
assessments.

Agroforestry systems, which integrate trees or shrubs with crops or
livestock, consistently outperform monocultures on both soil carbon and
biodiversity metrics. Alley cropping and silvopasture systems commonly show
20-40% higher species richness of birds and pollinators compared to adjacent
monoculture fields, along with 10-30% higher soil organic carbon due to
year-round root activity and leaf litter input.
Source: FAO Agroforestry Programme; World Agroforestry Centre (ICRAF)
synthesis studies.

Habitat fragmentation, where continuous natural habitat is broken into small
isolated patches by agriculture, roads, or development, reduces the
effective population size of many species below the threshold needed for
long-term genetic viability. Patches smaller than roughly 10 hectares
generally cannot sustain viable populations of area-sensitive species (e.g.
many forest-interior birds and mammals) even if habitat quality within the
patch is otherwise good.
Source: IPBES Global Assessment on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services.

Riparian buffer strips (vegetated corridors along waterways, typically 10-30
meters wide) filter agricultural runoff, stabilize stream banks, and create
continuous habitat corridors connecting otherwise fragmented land. Restoring
riparian buffers has been shown to reduce nitrogen and sediment loading into
waterways by 50-90%, directly benefiting aquatic species richness downstream.
Source: FAO land and water management guidance; EPA riparian buffer studies.

Intercropping (growing two or more crops in proximity, e.g. maize-legume or
cereal-legume systems) increases both above-ground structural diversity and
below-ground root diversity compared to monoculture. This is particularly
effective in semi-arid, low-rainfall regions where a companion legume can
improve soil nitrogen and water-use efficiency for the primary crop.
Source: FAO conservation agriculture and intercropping guidance.

Land use change from natural forest or grassland to cropland is the single
largest driver of terrestrial biodiversity loss globally, responsible for an
estimated 30% of global biodiversity decline according to recent
assessments. Reconverting even a modest fraction (10-15%) of marginal
cropland back to native vegetation or agroforestry can disproportionately
benefit landscape-level connectivity for wildlife.
Source: IPBES Global Assessment; CBD Global Biodiversity Outlook.

Crop rotation diversity (the number of distinct crop species grown in
sequence on the same field across seasons, as opposed to the same single
crop repeated) has an effect on soil-borne pest and pathogen pressure
independent of its soil-carbon benefits: rotating out of a host crop for
2-3 seasons breaks many pest life cycles, reducing the need for pesticide
inputs that would otherwise depress pollinator and soil arthropod
populations. This makes rotation diversity a rare intervention that touches
land-use, human-impact (pesticide reduction), and biodiversity metrics at
once.
Source: FAO Integrated Pest Management and crop rotation guidance.
