# Soil Health Knowledge Base

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is one of the strongest predictors of long-term soil
fertility and below-ground biodiversity. Degraded agricultural soils commonly
show SOC below 0.5%, while healthy temperate and tropical agricultural soils
typically range from 1.5% to 3%. Below roughly 1% SOC, microbial biomass and
water-holding capacity drop sharply, making land far more vulnerable to
drought stress and erosion.
Source: FAO Global Soil Partnership, soil organic carbon guidance.

Introducing legume-based cover crops (e.g. clover, cowpea, vetch) between or
alongside cash crops fixes atmospheric nitrogen and adds root biomass to the
soil. Field trials across semi-arid and temperate systems commonly report
soil organic carbon increases of roughly 15-25% over 2-3 years of sustained
cover cropping, along with measurable increases in microbial diversity and
pollinator visitation due to the additional flowering biomass.
Source: FAO conservation agriculture studies; USDA-NRCS cover crop trials.

No-till or reduced-till farming preserves soil structure and fungal networks
that are otherwise disrupted by ploughing. Long-term no-till plots typically
show 10-20% higher SOC than conventionally tilled plots after 5+ years, and
higher earthworm and soil arthropod counts, both of which are commonly used
as proxy indicators of soil biodiversity.
Source: FAO Conservation Agriculture Programme; peer-reviewed no-till
meta-analyses (e.g. Six et al., soil carbon sequestration literature).

Soil pH strongly constrains which plant and microbial communities can
establish. Most temperate crops and a majority of soil bacteria thrive
between pH 6.0 and 7.5; below pH 5.5, aluminum toxicity and phosphorus
lock-up become significant limiting factors, reducing both crop yield and
soil microbial diversity. Applying agricultural lime or biochar can raise pH
gradually over one to two growing seasons.
Source: FAO soil fertility management guidelines.

Soil moisture retention is closely linked to organic matter content: each
1% increase in soil organic matter can increase water-holding capacity by
roughly 15,000-20,000 liters per hectare in the top 30cm of soil. This
buffering capacity is critical in low-rainfall or semi-arid regions, where it
directly determines whether perennial vegetation and associated wildlife can
persist through dry seasons.
Source: USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service, soil-water relationships.

Compost and manure amendments increase both SOC and microbial diversity
faster than cover cropping alone, but at higher input cost and with a risk of
nutrient runoff if over-applied. A combined strategy of moderate compost
application plus cover cropping is generally recommended over either
practice alone for degraded soils with SOC below 1%.
Source: FAO integrated soil fertility management guidance.

Biochar amendment (charcoal produced by pyrolyzing crop residue at low
oxygen) is increasingly used to raise both SOC and cation exchange capacity
simultaneously. Because biochar is highly stable in soil (mean residence
time often exceeds 100 years, versus a few years for fresh organic matter),
it is one of the few soil amendments with a genuinely long-term carbon
sequestration case rather than just a fertility case — but it does little
for near-term biodiversity indicators like pollinator visitation, making it
a poor standalone choice when the goal is fast, visible improvement.
Source: FAO and IBI (International Biochar Initiative) joint technical
guidance.
